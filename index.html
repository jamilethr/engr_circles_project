Before beginning my undergraduate career at the University of Arkansas, I was completely unaware of the disparities in opportunities for students across the state. During my freshman year, I met several individuals from all over the state and got to hear about their upbringings and the resources they had access to when preparing for college. I myself originate from the southwest area of the state, and hearing my peers discuss the various opportunities they had in high school made me wonder if I was adequately prepared for college. From then, I questioned if the place an individual originated from had a significant impact on socioeconomic and educational outcomes of Arkansas residents. This led me to pursue a personal project that uses a data science approach to uncover disparities in post-secondary outcomes at the regional level. The project aims to better understand how educational factors such as educational attainment impact socioeconomic outcomes across regions.

        In the data science curriculum here at the University of Arkansas, we are taught to obtain guidance from the data science workflow. This process includes identifying a problem, acquiring data, data wrangling, data analysis, and communicating the data. I knew that following this workflow would ensure that my project ran smoothly and efficiently.

The most challenging aspect of this project came from acquiring the necessary data. Initially, the goal was to collect data that captured specific opportunities among Arkansas public high schools. To access this data, I sought sites such the ADE Data Center and ARData, but found that many schools, especially those in rural areas, were missing essential data that I would need for analysis. I then decided to access public datasets from the U.S. Census

Bureau. I found datasets that captured factors such as educational attainment, median earnings, and poverty status at the county level. This led me to reframe my guiding question in order to conduct a better, insightful analysis.

My guiding question became:

What is the relationship between regional location and post-secondary outcomes after holding educational and socioeconomic factors constant?​

To answer this question, I utilized the following datasets from the U.S. Census Bureau:

  • B99151 Allocation of Educational Attainment for the Population 25 Years and Over, American Community Survey
  • B23006 Educational Attainment by Employment Status for the Population 25 to 64 Years, American Community Survey
  • B20017 Median Earnings in the Past 12 Months (In 2021 Inflation-Adjusted Dollars) by Sex by Work Experience in the Past 12 Months for the Population 16 Years and over with Earnings in the Past 12 Months, American Community Survey
  • C17018 Poverty Status in the Past 12 Months of Families by Educational Attainment of Householder, American Community Survey

I combined data wrangling and feature engineering to filter for Arkansas counties and create metrics, such as employment and poverty rates. From there, I began conducting exploratory data analysis (EDA) and plotted educational attainment and region by median earnings along with educational attainment and region by poverty rate. From figure 1.1, it can be observed that the regional median earnings ranges widen as educational attainment increases​. It can also be observed that the central region of the states holds the least variance across educational tiers. Figure 1.2 shows a significant decrease in variance for households with a bachelor’s degree, demonstrating that regional location may lose its impact on poverty rate after obtaining a bachelor’s degree. Additionally, the southwest and southeast regions constantly observe the highest poverty rates for households with less educational attainment across educational tiers.

I also decided to create a choropleth map to geographically demonstrate the differences in median earnings and poverty rates across regions. Figure 1.3 demonstrates a slight, expected  inverse pattern of where median earnings are higher, poverty rate is lower and vice versa​.

Figure 1.3 Disparities by County and Region

After conducting exploratory data analysis, I decided to test whether the distributions observed were statistically significant (i.e. if regions differed and if so, which ones) by conducting a Kruskal-Wallis test and Mann-Whitney U tests. After conducting such tests, regional differences in median earnings among households with a bachelor's degree (H = 15.56, p = 0.004)​ and regional differences in poverty rates for households with a less-than-high school educational attainment (H = 14.37, p = 0.006)​ were found to be statistically significant. Regional differences in employment rates for households with a bachelor's degree was not found to be statistically significant (p = 0.867)​. In addition, statistical regional differences in median earnings are more generally observed between metro and non-metro areas​.

To test whether region has an independent impact, I used an Ordinary Least Squares regression model. Figure 1.4 shows the results of the OLS regression model. Based on the results from the previous tests, I believed it would be a good idea to use the central region as the baseline for comparison. The control variables used for this model were employment rate for those with a bachelor’s degree and poverty rate for those with an educational attainment less than high school. From this model, it can be observed that region has an independent effect on median earnings after holding educational and socioeconomic factors constant​.
